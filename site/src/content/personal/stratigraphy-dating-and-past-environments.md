A radiocarbon date on a charred grain cannot, by itself, tell us when a village disappeared. The grain might have burned in the year it was harvested, remained in storage for several years, or been moved from an older deposit into a pit dug much later. Even an accurate laboratory measurement leaves part of the historical question unanswered.

Establishing prehistoric time and environment requires three sets of relationships: how deposits formed in sequence, how the moment recorded by a sample relates to human activity, and how natural remains reflect living conditions. Each affects the next interpretation. Between identifying a mud layer as flood sediment and explaining a village's abandonment through flooding lie further questions about chronology, deposition, and human responses.

The preceding essay, [From Yangshao to Liangzhu: Reconstructing Prehistoric China](/personal/china-history/prehistory-sites-and-archaeological-evidence/?lang=en), considered how sites became subjects of research. This essay turns to excavation records and laboratory reports, following the connections between find locations, dates, and environmental material. The riverside settlement and sample labels used throughout are teaching examples. The discussions of Zhoukoudian and Liangzhu draw on published research.

## A settlement leaves an incomplete record of its life

Imagine a settlement on a river terrace. Residents level ground, build houses, cook, and dig pits outside. When a house deteriorates, new occupants dismantle walls, fill pits, and lay another floor. After abandonment, rain washes soil down the slope, and river water may bring further sediment. Much later, someone digs a grave on the same spot.

Excavation encounters the consequences of these activities. Some added deposits; others removed older material. Some intervals left no identifiable deposit. A thick layer might have formed rapidly in one flood, while a thin floor could have remained in use for years. Neither deposit thickness nor excavation depth translates directly into elapsed time.

This is why find position matters. A potsherd left on a floor during occupation supports a different argument from one mixed into pit fill centuries later. The first may be closely associated with occupation; the second initially establishes that older material was moved. Breakage, abrasion, orientation, surrounding sediment, and associated finds help researchers assess the relationship.

Stratigraphy investigates relationships among deposits and interfaces. An interface may be the edge created when someone excavated a pit, or a surface exposed by erosion. It need not be soil that can be collected in a bag, yet it records an action. Modern archaeological recording distinguishes deposits from interfaces so that cutting, using, and filling a pit remain separate events. [Harris's account of archaeological stratigraphy](https://harrismatrix.com/) describes the development of this approach.

## Establish the sequence of actions before dating the objects

Suppose the settlement first acquired floor A. Pit B was then cut through it, fill C accumulated inside the pit, and floor D eventually covered both the fill and the older floor. The established sequence is A before B, B before C, and C before D. Although the pit bottom lies below floor A, its fill formed later. Superposition must be interpreted within relationships that have not been disturbed.

A potsherd brought down from floor A into fill C was made before the filling event. Later roots or animal burrows can introduce younger material into older deposits. The former is residual material; the latter is intrusive. Both separate the statement that an object was found in a deposit from the claim that its age dates the deposit's formation.

Where two features have no direct relationship of superposition or cutting, their order may remain unresolved. Floors at the same depth need not have been laid simultaneously. Similar pottery in two pits may establish only a broadly comparable phase. Leaving an uncertain relationship unresolved prevents a subsequent chronological model from acquiring a sequence that was never observed.

In 1931, Liang Siyong's work at Hougang, Anyang, provided important stratigraphic evidence for the order of Yangshao, Longshan, and Xiaotun remains. Having studied archaeology at Harvard and joined Academia Sinica's Institute of History and Philology, Liang compared assemblages within their stratigraphic relationships. The result established a local relative sequence. It did not establish identical starting and ending years for comparable remains everywhere. [The institute museum's account](https://museum.sinica.edu.tw/en/knowledge-base/item/92/) presents the associated records.

## An ordered past still needs a calendar

Relative dating establishes sequence; numerical dating places events on a timescale expressed in years. They work together. Knowing that A preceded D does not establish whether ten or five hundred years separated them. Several laboratory dates without adequate sampling context can also turn surviving old charcoal into an apparent construction date.

The familiar term “absolute dating” means that results can be related to a numerical timescale. It promises neither a particular year nor freedom from uncertainty. A reported interval usually preserves more information than the single rounded number repeated in popular accounts. Sample identifiers, laboratory determinations, calibrated ranges, and estimates of historical events are distinct parts of a report.

Radiocarbon dating supplied an important timescale for remains without written dates. In 1949, James Arnold and Willard Libby published tests using samples of known age. Prehistoric research could compare an independent measurement with sequences established through artifact classification. [The publication record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15407879/) identifies the authors and date of that early validation.

Such work requires sustained laboratory provision. Peking University's School of Archaeology and Museology records that Chen Tiemei, Yuan Sixun, and Wang Liangxun began preparations in 1973, established a liquid scintillation radiocarbon laboratory in 1975, and introduced an accelerator mass spectrometry system in 1989. These investments connected field sampling with continuing chronological analysis. [The school's laboratory history](https://archaeology.pku.edu.cn/info/1366/4303.htm) describes the developments.

## Radiocarbon measures carbon in a sample

Carbon fourteen is a radioactive isotope of carbon. Plants acquire carbon through photosynthesis, and animals acquire it through food. After a living system stops exchanging carbon, its radiocarbon progressively decays. Measuring the remaining proportion, with an appropriate model, allows an estimate of the time since that carbon left biological exchange. [The American Chemical Society's explanation](https://www.acs.org/education/whatischemistry/landmarks/radiocarbon-dating.html) describes the reasoning.

If the target is a cooking event on floor A, a grain charred during that activity and preserved in place may be more closely associated with it than a large piece of charcoal of uncertain origin. Grain has a short growing life, but storage, movement, and mixing still require investigation. Charcoal might come from the interior of an old tree or a reused beam. The dated carbon can predate the fire: the familiar old wood effect.

Cleaning a sample and establishing its history are equally necessary. Laboratory pretreatment can remove some contamination, but cannot turn charcoal dug out of an old deposit into material newly formed when a pit was filled. Repeated measurements on the same poorly associated piece may agree while leaving that problem intact. Independent samples with clear associations help test the connection to the event. [Historic England's sampling guidance](https://historicengland.org.uk/images-books/publications/radiocarbon-dating-chronological-modelling/the-bayesian-process/) distinguishes these concerns.

Aquatic material introduces another difficulty. Some waters contain relatively old carbon, making shells or animals consuming aquatic resources appear older than contemporary terrestrial material. This reservoir effect varies with the water body, food sources, and material. Subtracting one fixed number of years is inadequate. Shells or human bone may require particular corrections and comparative samples. [The radiocarbon dating guidelines](https://historicengland.org.uk/images-books/publications/radiocarbon-dating-chronological-modelling/radiocarbon-dating/) explain the restrictions.

## BP does not translate directly into a BCE date

BP in a conventional radiocarbon age uses 1950 CE as its reference, regardless of publication year. It is also a radiocarbon age calculated according to a convention, rather than an already established calendar age. Atmospheric radiocarbon concentrations changed over time. Subtracting an uncalibrated determination from 1950 cannot supply an exact historical year.

Calibration connects radiocarbon ages with calendar dates through records with independent chronological controls. Results often comprise one or more intervals with associated probabilities. Bends and plateaus in the calibration curve can turn a precise laboratory measurement into a broad calendar range. Extra decimal places cannot repair that loss of chronological resolution. [Oxford's calibration explanation](https://c14.arch.ox.ac.uk/calibration.html) illustrates the relationship.

Pay attention to labels such as cal BCE and cal BP. The first expresses calibrated calendar years before the Common Era; the second expresses calibrated years before 1950. At whole year resolution, 5000 cal BP corresponds approximately to the vicinity of 3050 BCE. An uncalibrated determination of 5000 BP cannot be converted in the same way. Exact arithmetic across the era boundary must also account for the absence of a year zero.

Published in 2020, IntCal20 provides a radiocarbon calibration curve for Northern Hemisphere terrestrial samples. Researchers should identify the curve and version used; aquatic or other material may require different treatment. Updating a curve can change calendar ranges without changing the artifact itself. [The IntCal20 journal collection](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/radiocarbon/calibrations/intcal-20) lists the relevant curves and research.

## Older remains require a different clock

Radiocarbon has a limited dating range and cannot resolve every deposit hundreds of thousands of years old. Choosing a method begins with the processes experienced by a material and the event that needs dating. Results from different methods can be compared meaningfully only when their relationships to those events are understood.

Optically stimulated luminescence dating examines signals in minerals such as quartz to estimate time since their last sufficient exposure to light. River sand exposed during transport and subsequently buried may meet the necessary conditions. Inadequate exposure can leave an incompletely reset signal and produce an overestimate. The result primarily constrains sediment history; stratigraphy must connect it to nearby human activity. A [1985 optical dating paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/313105a0) proposed this approach, while [current methodological guidance](https://historicengland.org.uk/images-books/publications/scientific-dating-pleistocene-sites/scientific-dating-methods/) discusses its requirements and limits.

Uranium series methods can constrain the formation time of some cave carbonates. Where a carbonate deposit demonstrably overlies an archaeological deposit, its formation date can help bound the age of the underlying material. Researchers must also check whether material entering or leaving the sample has compromised the dating conditions. Dating one deposit in a cave does not directly date every artifact or fossil inside it.

In 2009, Shen Guanjun, Gao Xing, and colleagues applied cosmogenic aluminum twenty six and beryllium ten burial dating to lower deposits at Zhoukoudian Locality 1. They reported an age estimate of approximately 770,000 years for layers seven to ten. The method depends on nuclide production in quartz exposed to cosmic rays and subsequent changes in their ratio after burial. Interpreting exposure and burial history remains necessary. [The original paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature07741) identifies the samples and estimates.

**[Historical evidence remains disputed]** Here the disagreement concerns archaeological chronology. In the same year, Chen Tiemei and Zhou Liping compared chronological frameworks and identified problems requiring resolution between the burial ages and some existing stratigraphic interpretations. [Their paper](https://www.anthropol.ac.cn/EN/abstract/abstract1450.shtml) calls for measurements, correlations, and formation processes to be examined together. Zhoukoudian's entire record cannot be assigned one uniform, precise age.

## Turning several dates into a history of occupation

Return to the imaginary settlement. To estimate occupation duration, researchers need samples associated with its beginning, continuing use, and ending. Cooking remains on floor A and evidence from the last use of floor D constrain different parts of that history. Subtracting the earliest and latest results from two arbitrarily chosen charcoal fragments does not automatically establish occupation duration.

Overlapping calendar ranges do not, by themselves, demonstrate that two houses stood at the same time. The intervals express chronological uncertainty; the actual activities could fall at different positions within them. Conversely, if a gap is smaller than the method can resolve, a report should not present a sequence separated by several decades as established fact.

Chronological models can combine independently recorded sequence information with measurements. In Bayesian models, a prior expresses information explicitly adopted beyond those measurements, such as pit B cutting floor A. The model uses it to constrain possible sequences. Assuming that a flood caused abandonment and then treating the model output as proof of that cause would make the reasoning circular. Assumptions, outlier treatment, and sensitivity checks need to be available for scrutiny. [The chronological modelling guidance](https://historicengland.org.uk/images-books/publications/radiocarbon-dating-chronological-modelling/bayesian-chronological-modelling/) explains this combination of information.

Event boundaries often remain estimates. The latest dated sample can postdate earlier activity without establishing the day when all residents departed. People might have left gradually, the site might have changed use, or suitable samples from its final phase might not have survived. Occupation ending, structural destruction, and regional population change require separate assessments.

## Different environmental records cover different places

Environmental reconstruction investigates past vegetation, water conditions, landforms, and climate, often through proxies: surviving materials or properties associated with particular environmental processes. Lake sediments, pollen, charcoal, and cave stalagmites supply evidence, but observations require a mechanism linking them to an interpretation. [NOAA's introduction to proxy data](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/news/what-are-proxy-data) describes commonly used records.

Pollen helps identify past vegetation. Plants differ in pollen production, dispersal, and preservation; the area supplying a lake also depends on lake size and surrounding topography. A decline in one tree's pollen percentage may reflect reduced woodland or an increase in pollen from other plants. A percentage plot is not a direct measure of forest cover. Concentrations, deposition, and other plant remains matter. [The environmental remains guidance](https://historicengland.org.uk/images-books/publications/environmental-archaeology-3rd/heag327c-environmental-archaeology-appdx2/) discusses pollen and related material.

Charred seeds and wood charcoal often reflect human selection, transport, and use. One kind of charcoal inside a house does not establish that the surrounding woodland consisted entirely of that tree; it may represent selected fuel. Phytoliths are microscopic silica structures formed in plant tissues, with different preservation and transport properties. Comparison with pollen can help distinguish plants growing nearby from material brought into the settlement. [The archaeobotany introduction](https://historicengland.org.uk/research/methods/archaeology/archaeobotany/) identifies these categories.

Sediment grains, structures, and microscopic organisms help investigate water conditions, yet a fine mud layer can have several origins. Floodwater carries sediment, but lake expansion, slope wash, or longer periods of quiet water can also produce deposits requiring differentiation. Continuity, grain arrangement, erosion at boundaries, and distribution beyond one excavation unit offer more useful tests than color alone.

## Lake core depth still needs to be converted into time

Sampling a sediment core at successive depths reveals changes in its contents. When reliable dates exist at only some positions, ages between them must be estimated through an age depth model. Assuming uniform accumulation assigns similar time spans to equal thicknesses, despite possible acceleration, erosion, or missing sediment. [Blaauw and Christen's work on sediment chronology](https://pure.qub.ac.uk/en/publications/flexible-paleoclimate-age-depth-models-using-an-autoregressive-ga/) explains how assumptions about accumulation enter such models.

In the teaching example, sampling every centimeter would not justify declaring that each centimeter represents ten years. One flood might deposit several centimeters rapidly; a missing interval occupies no thickness. Without allowing for such processes, an apparently precise match between vegetation change and settlement abandonment could arise from interpolation. Adjacent curves need to be read with their respective chronological uncertainties.

Spatial correspondence also needs testing. A distant cave may record moisture changes across an area, but it is not a rain gauge outside the settlement. Moisture sources, rainfall seasonality, and hydrological processes above the cave can influence stalagmite isotope signals. Applying a regional record to one village requires compatible geography, recording mechanisms, and local deposits.

People also altered their surroundings. Vegetation loss might reflect drying, clearing, cultivation, or repeated burning. Fire remains can derive from human activities or natural fires. Lowland and elevated settlements face different risks under the same regional change; food supplies, buildings, and the organization of labor also influence outcomes. Environmental records help establish conditions for action, while occupation remains help identify responses.

## Testing the flood explanation at Liangzhu

Liangzhu occupied low terrain in the lower Yangtze region. Water and wetland resources supported life, while drainage difficulties and changing water levels could affect settlement and cultivation. It offers a useful example of the connections an environmental explanation needs. This section examines those connections without attempting a complete history of Liangzhu's political organization or demise.

In 2021, Zhang Haiwei, Cheng Hai, and colleagues compared cave stalagmite records with archaeological evidence. They argued that an unusually wet interval approximately 4,300 years ago supported an association between inundation and the abandonment of Liangzhu's ancient city. The caves lay outside the site, so the argument depended on chronology and regional hydrological relevance. [The original study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8626068/) presents an explanation requiring several types of evidence.

In the same year, He Keyang, Lu Houyuan, and colleagues examined relative sea level rise and marine inundation using sediments, microfossils, geochemistry, and modelling. Compared with an explanation emphasizing regional rainfall, it raises questions about water sources and processes that need comparison. [Their research](https://www.sciengine.com/parse/pdf/1674-7313/80710B2E6A8D43A39AA780B7506B31DC.pdf) likewise shows why additional indicators matter when identifying the origins of a silt layer.

**[Historical evidence remains disputed]** One record cannot establish every aspect of late Liangzhu environmental change or its effects on occupation at different sites. Similar timing justifies investigating a connection. A causal explanation also needs evidence about the water's character, event extent, damage to buildings or rice cultivation, and subsequent changes in human activity. City abandonment cannot establish that every resident across the region disappeared simultaneously.

The argument can be tested in four stages: whether regional records show an anomaly; whether that process affected the site; whether the impact coincided with the end of use; and whether material changes fit the proposed mechanism. Each stage can alter the explanation. This allows flooding to be investigated while making broad claims about climate and social disappearance answerable to their evidential requirements.

## Historical explanations must respect the scale of their evidence

These methods change the history that can be written. Reliable dates make regional comparison possible, while environmental remains place buildings, food, and activity locations within past conditions. The event recorded by a sample, the area represented by a record, and the duration a method can resolve determine how far an interpretation can extend.

A charcoal date may help constrain one activity; clearly recorded material compared across several locations may support a wider chronology. Flood sediment in one section can establish a local process. Regional migration requires settlement surveys and evidence of changing occupation elsewhere. Broader explanations require correspondingly broader evidence.

Archaeological records preserve neither everyone's name nor everyone's intentions. An explanation can investigate damage to houses, disruption to food production, and the conditions needed for relocation. It cannot supply an unrecorded leader with an invented speech announcing migration. Explaining material processes and the limits of inference already gives people without written records a place in history.

The grain's date can become part of a village's history only through its relationship with the floor, surrounding features, and associated samples. New measurements or another section may revise estimates of when occupation began, continued, and ended. Preserved samples and records make such revisions accountable to evidence.

## A timeline of methods and research

1. 1931 CE: Liang Siyong and colleagues obtain important stratigraphic evidence for the sequence of Yangshao, Longshan, and Xiaotun remains at Hougang.
2. 1946 CE: Libby proposes dating organic material through its radiocarbon content.
3. 1949 CE: Arnold and Libby publish tests of radiocarbon dating using samples of known age.
4. 1973 CE: Harris devises matrix recording of stratigraphic relationships; researchers at Peking University begin preparations for a radiocarbon laboratory.
5. 1975 CE: Peking University establishes a liquid scintillation radiocarbon laboratory.
6. 1979 CE: The first edition of Harris's Principles of Archaeological Stratigraphy appears.
7. 1985 CE: Huntley and colleagues publish their optical dating method for sediments.
8. 1989 CE: Peking University introduces an accelerator mass spectrometry radiocarbon system; the second edition of Harris's book appears.
9. 2009 CE: The Zhoukoudian burial dating study appears, followed that year by a comparison of chronological frameworks.
10. 2020 CE: The IntCal20 calibration studies are published.
11. 2021 CE: Liangzhu environmental studies examine regional wet conditions and marine inundation as processes associated with site changes.

These are dates of research, publication, or laboratory development, rather than dates of prehistoric events. Harris's editions are documented in [the author's bibliographic account](https://harrismatrix.com/about-the-book/); the other entries correspond to papers and institutional records linked above.

## People and their research connections

* Liang Siyong: An archaeologist working at the Institute of History and Philology whose Hougang fieldwork supplied evidence for a local relative sequence.
* Libby and Arnold: Collaborators in testing radiocarbon dating, bringing organic samples into independent numerical chronology.
* Harris: Developed systematic recording of sequences among deposits and interfaces for complex archaeological sites.
* Chen Tiemei, Yuan Sixun, Wang Liangxun, and colleagues: Helped build dating facilities at Peking University, connecting field questions with laboratory methods.
* Shen Guanjun, Gao Xing, and colleagues; Chen Tiemei and Zhou Liping: Published studies and comparisons addressing Zhoukoudian's chronological frameworks.
* Zhang Haiwei, Cheng Hai, and colleagues; He Keyang, Lu Houyuan, and colleagues: Investigated Liangzhu environmental change through different records.

These connections organize work around research questions and its place in the chain of interpretation. Except where collaboration is explicitly stated, they imply neither a teaching relationship nor participation in one project.
