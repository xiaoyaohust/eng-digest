Parts of the seabed beneath today's Yellow Sea and East China Sea lay above water when sea level was lower during the last glacial period. That ground could contain river channels, depressions, and usable resources. Meanwhile, water and vegetation in some areas now well inland had different distributions. Placing a modern map beside a prehistoric site can leave out part of the world its inhabitants encountered.

Pleistocene climate affected human life through specific geographical processes. Cooling altered whether snow survived the summer. Ice volume changed sea level. Monsoons changed where rain arrived. Water and vegetation then affected animal distributions, food seasons, and distances of travel. Each link had its own timing and regional variation. A temperature curve alone cannot identify a migration.

Following [Layers, Dates, and Past Environments](/personal/china-history/stratigraphy-dating-and-past-environments/), this essay moves from samples within sites to the geographical spaces in which people lived. References to sites within China use modern geography to locate evidence. Pleistocene activities did not follow today's national borders, and these societies had no political setting to which later dynastic institutions can simply be applied.

## Highlands inland regions and the ocean offered different conditions for life

Before the Pleistocene began, East Asia already contained high mountains, an interior far from the sea, and lowlands facing the western Pacific. The Tibetan Plateau and its surrounding ranges formed through long geological processes. A single ice age did not suddenly raise the plateau. Understanding Pleistocene environments begins with these more persistent conditions, then considers how cooling and warming acted upon them.

The ocean supplies moisture, but that moisture does not reach every part of the continent equally. Mountains obstruct and direct airflow. Air crossing ranges may lose some of its moisture, while the penetration of moist air into the interior varies with the seasons. Elevation also affects temperature, snow, and growing seasons. Plateaus, basins, valleys, and coasts at the same latitude consequently need not develop similar environments under the same global cooling.

For people living by gathering and hunting, these differences shaped daily possibilities. The length of winter, the freezing of water, the ripening of edible plants, and the seasonal movements of animals all affected where and when people could stay. Mean annual temperature captures only part of those conditions. A place with a short summer and long winter might offer abundant resources during its warm season while presenting a different subsistence problem during the rest of the year.

Mountains also played several roles. High ground could restrict movement, while valleys could allow people to travel along water sources. Vegetation zones at different elevations might provide resources at different seasons within a relatively small area. Representing an entire mountain region as an impassable wall, or a valley as a permanently open road, would erase these variations. ## Ice age rhythms arose from changing sunlight and feedbacks within the Earth system

In the International Commission on Stratigraphy's framework, the Pleistocene began approximately 2.58 million years ago and ended about 11,700 years ago, when the Holocene began. It was not a single uninterrupted interval of severe cold lasting more than two million years. Colder glacial periods alternated with relatively warm interglacials, and shorter fluctuations occurred within longer cold stages. The [International Chronostratigraphic Chart](https://stratigraphy.org/chart/) supplies the framework for these geological divisions.

Slow changes in the shape of Earth's orbit, the tilt of its rotational axis, and the relationship between the seasons and orbital position alter the sunlight reaching different latitudes and seasons. At high northern latitudes, whether summer melts the snow left from the previous year is particularly consequential. Snow that persists through many summers can gradually accumulate into thicker ice. The process requires sustained accumulation; one exceptionally cold winter cannot stand in for it.

As snow and ice expand, the surface reflects more solar energy, further affecting temperature. Ocean circulation, greenhouse gases, and the movement of ice itself modify the response. Orbital changes can therefore provide a rhythm, while several processes shape the outcome. [NASA's explanation of orbital cycles](https://science.nasa.gov/science-research/earth-science/milankovitch-orbital-cycles-and-their-role-in-earths-climate/) describes this connection between seasonal sunlight and ice volume.

Many Early Pleistocene ice volume changes were dominated by a rhythm of roughly 41,000 years. During the Mid Pleistocene Transition, approximately 1.25 million to 700,000 years ago, longer cycles with larger changes became more prominent. A rhythm of about 100,000 years is common in the Late Pleistocene. These numbers describe dominant timescales, not events that arrived punctually after a fixed number of years. [Simulations by Willeit and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6447376/) examine mechanisms including changing carbon dioxide and the removal of weathered material beneath ice sheets.

**[Historical evidence remains disputed]** The causes of the Mid Pleistocene Transition remain under discussion. Here the dispute concerns geological records and climate mechanisms. One set of simulations cannot be presented as a final explanation that excludes the others. What can be carried into this account is that the intensity and rhythm of glacial cycles changed. A single fixed cycle consequently cannot summarize the conditions encountered by East Asian populations throughout the Pleistocene.

## Monsoons changed where and when rain arrived

The East Asian monsoon can be approached as seasonally changing airflow. As the continent warms in summer, differences between land and ocean heating, together with the larger atmospheric circulation, favor the arrival of moist air over parts of the land. In winter, continental cooling makes cold air from the interior more prominent. The position of rainfall also depends on upper atmospheric westerlies, ocean temperatures, and terrain. One arrow pointing inland from the sea cannot explain it all.

Between glacial and interglacial conditions, ice volume, sea temperatures, and seasonal sunlight changed together, adjusting rain belts and moisture transport. In some northern regions, reduced warm season precipitation could limit vegetation and make water sources more scattered. Temperatures, rainfall seasonality, and evaporation in a southern basin or valley need not change in the same way. A few hundred kilometers can separate places with different terrain and moisture sources.

Less rain also need not shrink every lake at the same rate. Lake water balances depend on river inflow, groundwater, catchment area, and evaporation. Lower temperatures can reduce evaporation, while changes in snowmelt and glaciers can affect water supplies. Connecting colder glacial conditions to the drying of a lake beside a particular camp therefore requires local evidence.

In 2016, Cheng Hai, R. Lawrence Edwards, and colleagues extended a Chinese cave record across approximately 640,000 years, providing an important chronology for comparing monsoon changes with glacial terminations. Stalagmite oxygen isotopes also respond to moisture sources, rainfall along transport paths, and rainfall seasonality. They cannot simply be converted into annual millimeters of rain outside a cave. [Their study](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature18591) and [a comparison of East Asian runoff and cave records](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6105601/) help establish that distinction. An explanation of human life must eventually return to water and food in the region concerned.

## Northern loess records changes in dust and vegetation

Some of the fine material on the Loess Plateau arrived as windblown dust from drier regions. During certain intervals, dust inputs were greater and vegetation cover weaker. During others, relatively warm and moist conditions favored vegetation and soil development. Repeated processes left sequences of loess and ancient soils. A paleosol is not an automatically formed, tidy boundary marking every warm interval: winds, sediment sources, accumulation rates, and local soil formation all affect the record.

The work of Chinese Academy of Sciences geologists Liu Dongsheng, also published as Tungsheng Liu, and Ding Zhongli connected these terrestrial sequences with the study of past monsoons. Their questions concerned both the succession at a section and whether different places preserved comparable changes. Their [review of Chinese loess and the paleomonsoon](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.earth.26.1.111) discusses evidence for repeated advances and retreats of the rainfall belt during Pleistocene glacial cycles.

This provides a way to understand one constraint on northern populations. More dispersed water and food can change the costs of remaining in one place and of moving elsewhere. Greater dust accumulation cannot, by itself, establish that people immediately left. Valleys, springs, and local wetlands might retain conditions different from surrounding open ground. People might also alter the season or extent of their use of an area. These are possible responses to test against occupation evidence, rather than a single observed choice shared by every population.

The Nihewan Basin offers a specific geographical reference. Its Early Pleistocene sites are associated with former lake and river deposits, showing that the basin surface visible today underwent changes in water bodies and deposition. [Ao Hong and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3744199/) place the Shangshazui site at approximately 1.7 to 1.6 million years ago and discuss wider early human activity in the region. The example links human activity with environmental records. It cannot establish continuous occupation in every cold stage, or equate every sedimentary interruption with a population's disappearance.

## Falling sea level changed the amount of exposed land

The growth of large bodies of land ice stores more water on land, lowering global mean sea level. Deglaciation returns water to the ocean, raising that mean level. Near the Last Glacial Maximum, global mean sea level was roughly 120 to 130 meters below today's level, with some reconstructions producing lower estimates. Combining geological observations with models of Earth deformation, Lambeck and colleagues estimate a minimum of approximately 134 meters below the present level around 21,000 years ago. This is the result of a particular reconstruction, rather than an identical fall at every coast. [The original study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4217469/) explains the relationship between ice volume and estimated sea level.

An equal vertical fall can have very different effects on different coasts. A gently sloping continental shelf can expose extensive land, while a steep coast may experience a smaller horizontal shoreline shift. The shallow shelves of the Yellow Sea and East China Sea are consequently significant. Some lowlands available to prehistoric populations lie beyond the present coastline, and a modern coastal city need not have stood beside the sea at the time being studied.

The local height of the sea relative to the land is also affected by crustal movement, deformation caused by changing ice and water loads, and sediment compaction. Drawing one reconstructed East Asian shoreline directly from a value on a global curve would omit these processes. A detailed reconstruction requires local seabed topography, sediments, and relative sea level evidence. That is why no modern depth contour is labeled here as a securely established ancient coastline.

## Exposed shelves still contained rivers and barriers to movement

Retreating seawater did not leave a featureless plain without hydrological differences. Rivers could extend their lower courses seaward, while lowlands could contain new drainage networks, depressions, or wetlands. Whether exposed land was usable depended on fresh water, vegetation, food, flood risk, and seasonal conditions. Measuring the increase in land area does not establish an equal increase in suitable habitat.

The Sunda Shelf in Southeast Asia offers a comparatively clear example. Under lower sea levels, some regions now separated by shallow water could form connected land. [Voris's maps of Pleistocene Southeast Asia](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1046/j.1365-2699.2000.00489.x), constructed from bathymetry and sea level information, also show possible former river systems. Such maps establish how changing shores could alter regional connections. The configuration of the Sunda Shelf cannot simply be extended across all East Asian seas.

Exposing shallow seabed does not automatically eliminate deep straits. Connections around Taiwan, between the Korean Peninsula and the Japanese archipelago, and elsewhere require separate examination of strait depths, local crustal changes, and particular intervals. Representing one global lowstand as a time when every East Asian island joined the continent exceeds the evidence. Even where a passable land connection existed, demonstrating its actual use by people requires remains of the corresponding age.

The later drowning of land beyond today's coast also biases the archaeological distribution available to us. Scarcity of early sites along a modern shoreline may reflect a different former shoreline, burial, or erosion, rather than an absence of people. Preservation underwater is not uniformly better either. Marine erosion, sediment cover, survey methods, and sampling extent help determine what can be found today.

## River valleys combined resources with changing risks

Valleys can bring several resources close together: water, riverside plants, animals coming to drink, and stone suitable for tools. Rivers also move. Water levels and discharge vary seasonally, and a favorable occupation surface can be eroded by floods. A site close to a river today need not have occupied the bank of that same channel during its use.

Climate can change rivers through rainfall, freezing and thawing, vegetation, and sediment supply. Sea level can affect the base level for erosion downstream. Under particular conditions, some reaches incise while others accumulate sediment. Crustal movement and the transmission of sediment through a catchment also contribute. There is no single glacial water level timetable for every East Asian river, nor can the positions of modern deltas and tributaries be assumed to have persisted for hundreds of thousands of years.

Within one drainage system, basins, narrow valleys, and downstream plains can respond differently. Erosion in one reach can supply more sediment downstream; accumulation can in turn alter the location of channels and wetlands. Environmental effects therefore include shifts in the arrangement of familiar resources, alongside changes in how dry or wet a camp's surroundings become. Reconstructing the area used by a population requires attention to those relative positions.

Several human responses are possible. People might use the same place in different seasons, move their camps, enlarge the area from which they obtained resources, or change their food choices. Tools, food remains, and patterns of occupation can help distinguish these possibilities. A clear account of geography narrows the range of explanations without replacing behavioral evidence.

## The Last Glacial Maximum did not freeze all of East Asia at once

The Last Glacial Maximum commonly identifies an interval of large global land ice volumes and extensive ice sheets. Combining glacier chronology, Clark and colleagues place its main interval at approximately 26,500 to 19,000 years ago. Individual ice bodies did not all reach their maximum extent at the same time. The interval cannot be treated as a year when every mountain glacier advanced furthest. [Their study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19661421/) establishes a framework for global comparison.

Large ice sheets in North America and parts of Europe influenced global sea level and circulation. Most East Asian lowlands did not acquire equivalent continental ice cover. Mountain glaciers, seasonal snow, frozen ground, and cold surfaces without ice sheets are different geographical states. Their effects on movement, water, and biological resources must be distinguished.

The Tibetan Plateau particularly resists being colored as one uniform white block. Glacier growth requires sufficient snowfall as well as low temperature; colder but drier conditions need not produce more ice. Existing glacial landforms and dating do not support representing the plateau as covered throughout by one ice sheet at the Last Glacial Maximum. [Potential Refugia on the Tibetan Plateau during the Last Glacial Maximum](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/quaternary-research/article/potential-refugia-on-the-tibetan-plateau-during-the-last-glacial-maximum/92E451D57932778AA3A9B8AEB3C94192) compares glacier distributions and potentially suitable areas. Potential habitat does not itself establish human occupation.

Southern mountains also contained internal differences. Relatively low latitudes and varied terrain could preserve diverse local environments, but this does not establish that the entire south remained warm and wet, or that any population could move into it easily. Forests, open ground, valleys, and limestone regions offered different resources. Ways of living suited to one environment might require changes in another. Geographical proximity did not guarantee identical subsistence opportunities.

## Deglaciation rearranged resources while changing the coast

Deglaciation did not follow one smooth trajectory. Ice retreat, sea level rise, and atmospheric and oceanic adjustments operated at different rates. While global mean sea level broadly rose, the North Atlantic region experienced renewed cold episodes. Temperatures and precipitation across East Asia must still be examined regionally. Warming in one place did not create the same moist environment everywhere on the same day.

In lowlands, rising seas could drown former ground and shift river mouths inland, while also accompanying the formation of new bays, lagoons, and coastal resources. The loss of an existing occupation area and the appearance of new resources could follow one another in the same region. Responding to those changes involved rearranging food procurement and travel distances. The effects cannot be reduced to beneficial sea retreat and harmful sea advance.

The rate of sea level rise also affects how many generations experienced a geographical change. Lambeck and colleagues reconstruct a relatively rapid rise approximately 14,500 to 14,000 years ago, followed by changes in the rate. This global water volume signal still cannot identify the year a particular East Asian camp was inundated. Local topography, relative sea level, and site chronology must be considered together to assess that possibility.

The Holocene began approximately 11,700 years ago. Environmental changes supplied new conditions for later settlement and agriculture without prescribing one outcome. Plant availability, food processing, occupation patterns, and connections among populations each require their own evidence. The origins of agriculture belong to later essays in this series and cannot be explained in advance by a single warming event.

## Climate becomes human history through specific living conditions

The processes above form a concrete explanatory sequence. Seasonal sunlight and feedbacks altered ice volume and circulation. Sea level and moisture transport changed in response. Local water sources, vegetation, and animal resources then changed, and people acted within those conditions. At every step, terrain, differences in timing, and existing ways of living could affect what followed.

For example, reduced warm season water might increase travel to a water source. A reliable nearby spring would make the consequences different from those at a place without one. If edible resources shifted primarily between seasons, people might change the timing of visits rather than leave permanently. These are plausible mechanisms; whether they operated at a particular site requires local evidence.

**[Historical evidence remains disputed]** Identifying a particular climate fluctuation as the direct cause of a specific migration often remains difficult. Chronological uncertainty, survey gaps, differences in preservation, and other possible responses all affect the judgment. Models of environmental suitability can suggest areas to investigate. They cannot independently identify a particular group's origin and destination. Questions about hominin identities and genetic relationships also require fossil and genetic evidence.

Today's mountains and rivers preserve only part of the spaces used by Pleistocene populations. Some former land lies underwater. Rivers changed position, and resource distributions repeatedly shifted. Understanding those changes allows us to distinguish an undiscovered site, an area unsuitable for occupation, and a population that demonstrably left. As the series turns to early human remains, their geographical conditions must belong to the periods in which those people lived.

## Timeline

These approximate ages summarize geological and environmental changes without assigning every event to an exact year. BP in Figure 3 is measured from 1950 CE. The formal Holocene boundary uses 11,700 years before 2000 CE; the discussion uses approximate ages at the scale of many millennia.

1. Approximately 2.58 million years ago: The Pleistocene begins, establishing the geological span of this essay.
2. Approximately 1.7 to 1.6 million years ago: Activity at Shangshazui in the Nihewan Basin is associated with Early Pleistocene basin deposits.
3. Approximately 1.25 million to 700,000 years ago: During the Mid Pleistocene Transition, longer glacial cycles with greater changes become prominent.
4. Approximately 125,000 years ago: A commonly used reference interval within the last interglacial, with global ice volume and sea level markedly different from those at the Last Glacial Maximum.
5. Approximately 26,500 to 19,000 years ago: The main Last Glacial Maximum interval, with extensive ice in many regions and low global sea level.
6. Approximately 20,000 to 19,000 years ago: Northern Hemisphere deglaciation begins to advance, followed by an overall rise in sea level.
7. Approximately 14,500 to 14,000 years ago: Sea level reconstructions record relatively rapid rise, with differing local relative sea level responses.
8. Approximately 11,700 years ago: The Pleistocene ends and the Holocene begins. People continue living amid changing coasts, water sources, and vegetation.

## Researchers and their connections to the evidence

* Liu Dongsheng and Ding Zhongli: Studied and synthesized loess, paleosols, and paleomonsoon records, connecting northern terrestrial sediments with climate history.
* Cheng Hai, R. Lawrence Edwards, and collaborators: Used stalagmite chronologies and oxygen isotope sequences to investigate monsoon changes and glacial terminations.
* Rachel Spratt and Lorraine Lisiecki: Combined marine sediment reconstructions to produce the sea level series used in Figure 3.
* Peter Clark and collaborators; Kurt Lambeck and collaborators: Investigated, respectively, the timing of extensive ice cover and the relationships among ice volume, sea level, and Earth deformation.
* Ao Hong and collaborators: Applied paleomagnetic chronology to Shangshazui in the Nihewan Basin, placing human activity within regional sedimentary and temporal frameworks.
