A mandible can preserve teeth and the structures used in chewing without yielding recoverable DNA. A fragment too small to reveal the shape of a skull can preserve enough molecular information to identify a previously unrecognized population. Advances in the study of ancient people can therefore change the questions that a specimen answers without making the specimen more complete.

The Xiahe mandible study published in 2019 provides one example. Ancient proteins connected this fossil from Gansu with Denisovans. Several years later, the Harbin cranium entered discussions involving morphological classification, ancient proteins, and mitochondrial DNA. Skeletal and molecular records can test one another, while supplying different kinds of information.

Studying prehistoric populations within China requires separate judgments about an individual's physical characteristics, its relationships to known samples, and whether those findings support a wider population history. Following [Changing Climates and Shifting Shores in Pleistocene East Asia](/personal/china-history/pleistocene-climate-and-east-asian-geography/), this essay examines how those judgments are made. Place names locate evidence here. They do not assign Pleistocene populations to modern ethnic or national identities.

## Skeletal morphology provides physical evidence for comparison

Hominin fossils include teeth, jaws, skulls, and remains from other parts of the body. They initially preserve information about particular individuals. Tooth shape and wear, skeletal dimensions and structure, and signs of injury or development can help researchers investigate classification, growth, and life experiences. Preservation differs between body parts. A tooth and a more complete skeleton do not answer identical questions.

Morphological comparison commonly examines combinations of characteristics. A particular brow ridge, large teeth, or thick bone need not be exclusive to one population. Variation exists within populations, while age, sex, development, and disease also affect form. Turning one conspicuous feature into a species label can neglect other evidence. [The original classification paper for the Harbin cranium](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8454552/) illustrates the use of multiple features to propose a classification.

Burial can also compress, break, or remove parts of a fossil. Physical assembly and digital reconstruction can restore structures for comparison, but require decisions about how gaps should be filled and fragments positioned. An angle or proportion in a reconstruction may reflect both the preserved specimen and the reconstruction method. Directly preserved structures must be distinguished from estimated ones; the surfaces of a three dimensional model do not all carry equal evidential weight.

These limits leave morphology essential. Even when molecular information is recovered, the fossil identifies the body part sampled, developmental characteristics, and the specimen's place among other remains. Bones can preserve traces of use that molecular results do not directly explain. This division of work makes it possible to check a classification with different evidence.

## Molecular survival depends on the conditions of burial

DNA carries genetic information. Living cells maintain and repair it, but those processes stop after death. Molecules fragment and undergo chemical changes. Ancient DNA research often works with short, scarce fragments mixed with material from other sources. Researchers recover comparable information from these fragments rather than extracting a complete, undamaged genetic instruction book from a bone.

Preservation depends on temperature, moisture, microbial activity, and the material itself, among other conditions. Two fossils of the same age can retain very different amounts of molecular information. Different parts of one specimen can also yield different results. Cold, stable environments often favor preservation, but there is no single DNA expiry date applicable to every fossil. [Dabney, Meyer, and Pääbo's review of ancient DNA damage](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3685887/) explains fragmentation and chemical alteration.

Proteins can also preserve sequence information suitable for comparison. They consist of amino acids, and some proteins within particular structures of bones or teeth can sometimes survive longer than DNA. Researchers use methods such as mass spectrometry to identify fragments and compare informative sequence differences. The comparison concerns surviving protein sequences; it does not recover an entire genome. Proteins also deteriorate, and different proteins discriminate relationships at different levels.

Differences in preservation affect the distribution of available samples. If some environments preserve DNA more readily, more sequenced genomes may come from those places. A blank area on a map can mean undiscovered remains, unsampled material, or failed preservation. It cannot immediately establish that no people lived there. The environmental conditions discussed in the previous essay thus also influence which populations research can observe.

## Molecules entering the laboratory need evidence of their origin

Excavated remains can encounter excavators, curators, packaging, and laboratory surroundings. Modern human DNA shares many similarities with DNA from ancient people, so contamination can affect results. Clean sampling, laboratory blanks, and separation of different stages of work form part of the foundation of analysis. Human fragments read by a sequencer cannot all be assigned to the fossil's original owner.

Authentication also examines the molecules. Fragment length distributions and characteristic damage at fragment ends can supply evidence, including changes in readings caused by alterations to cytosine at some terminal positions. Short fragments or one damage pattern cannot independently guarantee authenticity. Laboratory treatment can change the visible damage as well. Contamination estimates, controls, and reproducibility must be considered together when judging whether the retained data support an interpretation. [Orlando and colleagues' methods primer](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43586-020-00011-0) describes these stages.

More sequencing reads do not automatically mean more independent information. Amplification of a small set of molecules can generate many duplicate reads. Without distinguishing duplicates from independent fragments, large numbers may overstate the evidence available. Likewise, failure to read a position differs from observing a particular genetic state there. Coverage and error must be evaluated alongside a proposed relationship.

Reference samples also shape interpretation. Fragments are commonly compared with known genomes, which do not represent every population that once existed. Finding the closest match among known samples describes a result within the current comparison. It does not exclude every unsampled population. Additional references and improved methods may subdivide an initially broad inference.

## Maternal lineages and nuclear genomes trace different relationships

Most human genetic information resides in the cell nucleus, with a smaller set of DNA in mitochondria. Human mitochondrial DNA usually passes through the maternal line, making it useful for tracing one particular line of descent. Nuclear DNA comes from both parents and is reshuffled through recombination across generations, retaining contributions from a broader set of ancestors. Both concern relationships, but cover different paths.

A person has a mother and a father, with further ancestors behind each. Following only the mother and maternal grandmother does not simultaneously record the other ancestors. Even a complete mitochondrial genome retains that limitation. Completeness describes how thoroughly this set of molecules has been read, rather than the recovery of every ancestral relationship.

Nuclear DNA offers many more positions for investigating relationships, population divergence, and gene flow. Results still depend on the positions actually read, sample ages, and comparative models. The common ancestor of particular genetic segments need not date to the moment two populations began separating. Different positions can retain different histories, while later exchange changes the overall pattern.

When mitochondrial and nuclear DNA suggest different relationships, the first questions concern the inheritance paths, samples, and statistical support involved. Disagreement does not immediately invalidate either category of evidence. The [public description of the high coverage Denisovan genome](https://www.eva.mpg.de/genetics/genome-projects/denisova/) separately reports mitochondrial and nuclear contamination estimates, illustrating the need to check the two kinds of information individually.

## Denisovans changed how an ancient population could be recognized

Denisova Cave in the Altai Mountains of Siberia connects evidence within China to a wider Eurasian discussion. A small finger bone discovered in 2008 later supplied genetic material. Genome research published in 2010 identified an archaic population with relationships distinct from those of Neanderthals and modern humans. Recognition did not require a skeleton revealing the complete appearance of its owner. [Reich and colleagues' original study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4306417/) records that change.

Denisovans consequently began primarily as a population recognized through molecular relationships. This differs from names first assigned through skull morphology and subsequently tested with molecular evidence. Connecting later jaws, teeth, or crania to this group requires further comparisons. Uncertainty about Denisovan morphology cannot justify assigning every difficult Asian fossil to the category.

In 2018, Slon and colleagues' analysis of another bone fragment from Denisova Cave supported an individual with a Neanderthal mother and a Denisovan father. This supplied specific evidence of parentage, making reproductive contact between different archaic populations more directly observable. [The study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0455-x) demonstrates the possibility of gene flow after divergence.

The finding challenges diagrams of human evolution whose branches separate and never reconnect. Divergence and exchange can coexist. One individual of mixed parentage, however, cannot establish contact rates in every region. Moving from that individual's parentage to the locations, dates, and scale of repeated exchange between populations requires more material.

## Xiahe placed molecular relationships in a specific geographical setting

Baishiya Karst Cave near Xiahe in Gansu lies on the northeastern Tibetan Plateau. In 2019, Chen Fahu, Zhang Dongju, Frido Welker, Jean-Jacques Hublin, and collaborators published a study of a mandible from the site. Although no ancient DNA suitable for classification was recovered from the specimen, surviving protein sequences connected it with Denisovans. [The original paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1139-x) reports a minimum age of approximately 160,000 years, based on uranium series dating of adhering carbonate.

A minimum age differs from the exact time an individual lived. The relationship between carbonate formation and the bone's entry into its surroundings needs interpretation. At least approximately 160,000 years cannot become exactly 160,000 years in retelling. Such limits affect whether a fossil can be assigned to a particular environmental stage and whether different remains at a site represent contemporary activity.

In 2020, Denisovan mitochondrial DNA from cave sediments added a different source of evidence. [Zhang and colleagues' paper](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abb6320) associates relevant layers with approximately 100,000 and 60,000 years ago, and possibly later activity. Sedimentary molecules support a connection between populations and this environment. They do not automatically identify the maker of every tool or turn different layers into a record of one household's uninterrupted residence.

In 2024, protein analysis assigned a rib fragment from the cave to Denisovans, with its layer dated to approximately 48,000 to 32,000 years ago. [Xia Huan and colleagues' study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07612-9) added skeletal evidence that could be related to archaeological context. The mandible, sedimentary DNA, and rib supply evidence from different times and materials. Connecting them does not fill every generation in a continuous population history.

## Harbin brought a morphological name into comparison with molecular results

The relatively complete Harbin cranium permits comparison of many cranial features. In 2021, Ji Qiang, Ni Xijun, and colleagues used its combination of characteristics to propose the species name Homo longi. This was a taxonomic proposal concerning the shared characteristics of a group of fossils and their relationships to named specimens. Publication of the name left it open to tests using other evidence.

Two studies in 2025 changed the available comparisons. [Ancient proteins reported in Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adu9677) linked the Harbin individual with known Denisovan reference material. [Research published in Cell](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2025.05.040) recovered mitochondrial DNA from dental calculus, also supporting Denisovan affinity. Agreement between different molecular sources added support beyond an isolated morphological resemblance.

The scope of that information still matters. Mitochondrial DNA from calculus is not a complete nuclear genome from the cranium. Protein comparisons cover the sequences actually recovered. Approximately 146,000 years is a minimum age constraint, rather than an exact date. The results strengthen the connection of this individual with Denisovans without providing equivalent molecular evidence for every other fossil resembling it.

**[Historical evidence remains disputed]** Questions remain between molecular affinity and species nomenclature. Which specimens the name Homo longi should include, how Denisovan lineages should be subdivided, and whether particular morphological differences justify separate species depend on classification criteria and comparative samples. One cranium's affinity cannot be restated as a unique, universally accepted resolution of every disputed name.

## New evidence can revise classifications while leaving specimens unresolved

Fossil research does not begin with a complete list of species into which every discovery must fit. Additional specimens may reveal more variation in features previously thought diagnostic. Material once treated as a heterogeneous group may separate into different relationships. Molecular work expands opportunities for testing while retaining gaps in its references.

A Guangxi study published on November 18, 2026 provides a recent example. Researchers reported nine hominin teeth from Naxian and Baxian caves near Chongzuo, within an overall chronological framework spanning approximately 324,000 to 184,000 years ago. Five teeth underwent protein analysis; four contained sequence evidence supporting attribution to Homo erectus. The assignment of another tooth, PA1796, remained unresolved. [Liao, Zanolli, Welker, and colleagues](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11271-3) did not classify every different tooth as Denisovan.

Stronger classification for some specimens can coexist with uncertainty about another. Overlapping dates do not establish that different populations met on the same day. Possible presence of another lineage cannot be expanded into evidence of its interbreeding with a particular group. For such recent work, distinguishing what the paper establishes from the possibilities it discusses is more useful than repeating a tidy list of names.

## Available samples do not evenly represent past populations

Progress in molecular research can give the impression that ancient genomes already cover the map adequately. In practice, discovery, preservation, sampling permission, and laboratory success jointly determine the available material. Greater study of one region may reflect more suitable remains or more extensive investigation and laboratory work. An absence of results elsewhere may have several explanations.

The deep history of modern humans also requires ancient specimens for testing. In 2013, Fu Qiaomei and colleagues published DNA analysis of an approximately 40,000 year old individual from Tianyuan Cave near Beijing, recovering mitochondrial and partial nuclear information. [The original paper](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1221359110) introduced an ancient person with a location and age into the comparison. This supplied a direct reference beyond inferences from living populations. It remained one individual, rather than a representative of everyone then living within China.

Ancestry components in statistical analyses also depend on the references and models chosen. If an important ancient population has not been sampled, a model may use a relatively close available reference to represent it. The colored proportions in a figure need not correspond to a past tribe whose name, boundaries, and composition are independently known. Colors must be interpreted through model definitions and the scope of the material.

**[Historical evidence remains disputed]** More than one reconstruction of population divergence, migration direction, or exchange scale may explain current data. If samples from another place or period could change a result, its comparative conditions matter. Research can narrow the range of possibilities without filling gaps with unsupported migration arrows.

## Affinity and population history require separate arguments

An individual closer to a set of reference samples can support a relationship inference. Establishing how long that relationship persisted within a region requires more samples across places and periods. Language, institutions, and shared identities require their own archaeological or written evidence. Expanding the wording of one genetic inference cannot answer all those questions.

Modern ethnic names particularly cannot be assigned directly to fossils hundreds of thousands of years old. Shared ancestors, the survival of some genetic segments, language transmission, and the formation of collective identities operate across different timescales and processes. A lineage's genetic contribution does not establish that its descendants continuously spoke one language or maintained the same social organization. Biological relationships do not reduce cultural history to a single line of descent.

Reconstructions of appearance likewise require attention to their evidence. Bone can constrain some physical characteristics, while hair, skin surfaces, expressions, and clothing often involve additional information or artistic choices. Even a scientific reconstruction needs to distinguish directly preserved structures from estimates. A vivid face cannot become retrospective proof of a relationship or cultural assignment.

Sampling itself changes a fossil. Some molecular analyses consume material, making sampling location, existing documentation, and the question being tested consequential. Failure to detect DNA can prompt consideration of another tissue or protein analysis, but further sampling cannot guarantee success. A rare specimen is material for the current experiment and a possible subject for future methods.

Xiahe and Harbin have brought formerly difficult specimens into better supported comparisons of affinity. They have not supplied every missing population or restored undocumented excavation information. As the series turns to Yuanmou, Lantian, and other remains, a fossil's name needs to be read alongside the evidence it preserves and the questions it still leaves open.

## Timeline of research

The following are discovery or publication dates. They differ from the prehistoric ages of the specimens and should not be read as a chronology of population movements.

1. 2008 CE: A small finger bone later used in molecular research is discovered at Denisova Cave.
2. 2010 CE: Denisovan molecular studies identify a previously unrecognized archaic population.
3. 2012 CE: A high coverage Denisovan genome is published, supplying an important reference for later comparisons.
4. 2013 CE: DNA analysis of the approximately 40,000 year old modern human from Tianyuan Cave is published.
5. 2018 CE: Research on Denisova 11 supports direct parentage from a Neanderthal mother and Denisovan father.
6. 2019 CE: Ancient proteins link the Xiahe mandible with Denisovans.
7. 2020 CE: Denisovan mitochondrial DNA from Baishiya sediments is reported.
8. 2021 CE: A study proposes the name Homo longi using the Harbin cranium's morphology.
9. 2024 CE: Research on a rib fragment and associated faunal remains from Baishiya is published.
10. 2025 CE: Separate studies report Harbin proteins and mitochondrial DNA from dental calculus.
11. November 2026 CE: Teeth from Naxian and Baxian caves in Guangxi add evidence concerning Homo erectus while leaving some classification questions open.
