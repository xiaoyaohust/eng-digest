Volume I, Early Humans and Prehistoric Societies within China. Chapter 2, Early Humans in China. Section 2.

Zhoukoudian matters because human remains, tools, animals, and sediments survive together at one locality. They allow researchers to investigate early human anatomy alongside the environment of human activity. When Pei Wenzhong discovered a relatively complete Peking Man skullcap at Dragon Bone Hill on December 2, 1929, years of investigation had already prepared the way. Excavation, study, and interpretation continued for almost a century afterward.

The familiar image of Peking Man often depicts a fixed scene: people making tools by the cave entrance, tending a fire inside, and sharing animals brought home from the hunt. The evidence assembled into that picture can come from different layers, periods, and processes of accumulation. Carnivores gnawed some bones; water transported some sediments. Fire research moved from early acceptance through challenges to renewed excavation and laboratory testing.

Understanding Zhoukoudian requires following how discoveries expanded comparisons and how new questions changed earlier reconstructions of daily life. Continuing from [Yuanmou and Lantian in the Early Human Record of China](/personal/china-history/yuanmou-lantian-and-early-human-presence/), this installment focuses on Peking Man at Locality 1 and the study of Homo erectus. Later material from the Upper Cave belongs to a different period. Its ornaments, burials, and anatomical features cannot be assigned collectively to Peking Man.

## Dragon Bone Hill stands where mountains meet the plain

Zhoukoudian lies in Fangshan, southwest of present day central Beijing. Near the transition from mountains to plain, limestone fissures and caves provided spaces in which bones and sediments could accumulate. Locality 1 originally formed part of a cave system. Roof collapse and exposure of its deposits subsequently changed its appearance. Today's site differs from the setting in which ancient humans were active.

This position mattered both to resources and to preservation. Different terrain could supply stone, animals, and plants, while caves could be used by humans and other animals at different times. Nearby mountains, plains, and water systems offered varied settings without implying that every period had today's climate, vegetation, or water supply. Particular activities still require evidence from their corresponding horizons.

Caves protect materials while also modifying their context. Water can bring in fine particles; rocks can fall from the walls and roof; bones may enter deposits after exposure elsewhere. A tool finally lying beside an animal bone need not have started there. Zhoukoudian's richness provides more opportunities to test relationships while making a simple picture of residence insufficient.

Zhoukoudian also comprises several localities. Locality 1 is central to the classic Peking Man fossil and tool record; the Upper Cave preserves later modern human material; other localities have their own deposits. [UNESCO's site description](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/449) presents a complex containing evidence from multiple periods. A shared place name does not establish contemporaneity or a single culture throughout.

Unless otherwise indicated, the cave, fire, and Homo erectus discussed here concern Locality 1. Identifying the locality comes before relating a particular bone, tool horizon, and age.

## Teeth became the basis of a continuing research project

Zhoukoudian research began within modern geological and paleontological surveying. In the early twentieth century, the Geological Survey of China developed field investigation, collections, and scientific publication. Chinese geologists including Ding Wenjiang and Weng Wenhao worked with collaborators such as the Swedish geologist Johan Gunnar Andersson. Collecting mammalian fossils brought Zhoukoudian into this research program.

Austrian paleontologist Otto Zdansky participated in early excavations in 1921 and 1923, and some material was later studied in Sweden. The Museum of Evolution at Uppsala University [describes the collaboration and its surviving Peking Man teeth](https://www.uu.se/en/museum-of-evolution/our-collections/palaeontological-collections/lagrelius-collection). Discovery, identification, and publication did not necessarily occur in the same year. Different short histories can consequently mean different events when assigning a discovery date.

In 1927, Canadian anatomist Davidson Black of Peking Union Medical College proposed the name Sinanthropus pekinensis on the basis of Zhoukoudian dental material. The human sample remained small, so classification required testing against further discoveries. [A contemporary discussion of the new skulls in Nature in 1937](https://www.nature.com/articles/139261a0) looked back on the progression from a classification based on a tooth to tests using more crania.

A tooth could establish a question; sustained excavation required organization. Cooperation between the Geological Survey and the medical college connected stratigraphy, anatomy, specimen preparation, and funding. The Cenozoic Research Laboratory, established in 1929, provided an institutional base. [Research history preserved by the IVPP](https://www.ivpp.cas.cn/zt/zt/qzjs80zn/sqjnwj/201811/P020181103622158990424.pdf) records the contributions of Weng, Black, and Yang Zhongjian.

A significant discovery depends on the ability to preserve its information as well as the opportunity to find it. Cleaning, numbering, comparison, and publication make teeth and skulls available for scrutiny by others. A continuing collaborative project allowed isolated human finds to develop into a series in which population variation could be investigated.

## Pei Wenzhong and Jia Lanpo expanded the fossil series

Pei Wenzhong graduated in geology from Peking University and joined the Geological Survey before working at Zhoukoudian. He was still a young field researcher in 1929. The skullcap discovered on December 2 preserved cranial structures unavailable from the earlier dental material. [The IVPP's account of Pei](https://www.ivpp.cas.cn/kxcb/kpdt/202401/t20240123_6963585.html) records the discovery date and his scientific career.

The specimen allowed earlier classification to be checked using connected structures. Researchers could compare the frontal region, brow, vault, and occipital region without asking teeth to support every inference. A relatively complete skullcap still differs from a complete head. Understanding the face, lower jaw, and rest of the body required other evidence.

Removal from the deposits did not complete the discovery. Hardened matrix needed careful preparation; fragments required positioning; specimen identities had to remain linked to stratigraphy. Preparators and field workers directly affected which details survived. A history remembering only the person who lifted a fossil conceals labor that made it usable evidence.

Jia Lanpo joined the Cenozoic Research Laboratory in 1931, participated in excavation, and later succeeded Pei in directing fieldwork. In November 1936, he discovered three Peking Man skullcaps in succession. [The IVPP's biographical account](https://www.ivpp.cas.cn/zt/zt/qzjs80zn/sqjnwj/201811/P020181103622232467174.pdf) records his changing responsibilities and these discoveries. Multiple specimens allowed shared features to be distinguished from individual differences and made the peculiarities of any one fossil easier to assess.

After Black died in 1934, German anatomist Franz Weidenreich continued systematic study of Peking Man. Descriptions, measurements, drawings, and casts brought the finds into wider anatomical comparisons. Linking field discoveries with laboratory study helped Zhoukoudian become a reference locality for Asian Homo erectus. This took years and cannot be reduced to one day's discovery of a complete ancestor.

## Peking Man became part of the study of Homo erectus

The name Sinanthropus pekinensis arose when fossils and comparative samples were limited. Similarities between Zhoukoudian and other material, including the Javanese fossils, later entered systematic comparison. Peking Man is now generally assigned to Homo erectus. Changes of name reflect a broader classification framework applied to the specimens.

Commonly discussed Zhoukoudian features include a relatively long, low cranium, prominent brow structures, and characteristics of bone thickness and form in different regions. They require comparison as combinations of features. A prominent brow alone does not establish a species; age, sex, preservation, and reconstruction can also affect measurements. [The Smithsonian's Peking Man description](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/fossils/zhoukoudian) specifically identifies a familiar composite skull as a combination of several individuals from the site. It is not one person's complete original skull.

Cranial capacity and internal form also enter research, without supplying an intelligence score. An endocast records the shape of the cranial cavity. It does not directly reveal language, memory, or an individual's thoughts. [Research on the endocast of Zhoukoudian Skull V](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2842660/) illustrates comparisons among specimens. Behavioral inferences need additional evidence; ordering brain volumes does not independently order the capacities of ancient people.

Homo erectus also does not identify the moment when upright walking began. It is a historically developed classification covering a long interval and large area. [A comparative overview of Homo erectus](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-erectus) describes geographical and morphological variation. Zhoukoudian provides an important reference without establishing identical anatomy and technology for all members at all sites and times.

Its value is therefore specific. A series available for repeated comparison helps investigate the distribution of shared features. That contribution does not require every later East Asian population to be placed on a single line of descent from the site.

## The revised age concerns particular layers

Different generations of books give different ages for Peking Man. Some numbers concern a layer at Locality 1, others summarize a longer interval, and others retain earlier dating. The object being dated must be identified before the numbers are compared. The deposits did not all form on one day.

In 2009, Guanjun Shen, Xing Gao, Bin Gao, and Darryl Granger published aluminum 26 and beryllium 10 burial dating of Locality 1. Measurements of quartz sediments and stone artifacts from its lower part supported a best estimate of approximately 770,000 years for Layers 7 to 10, with a reported uncertainty of about 80,000 years. This is the standard error of the weighted mean of six meaningful ages, rather than the age spread of all Peking Man individuals. [The original paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature07741) related this result to paleomagnetism and other chronological evidence.

An age older than some familiar estimates changed the environmental context. Researchers needed to consider human activity during relatively cold northern conditions. Dating older horizons does not demonstrate year round uninterrupted residence in one cave. Temperature alone cannot establish a particular method of keeping warm. Environmental pressure raises a question; behavior still needs evidence from the site.

The approximately 770,000 year estimate also cannot be transferred to all the Layer 4 fire evidence discussed below. Each horizon requires its own chronological relationships. Combining the oldest age with the clearest evidence from a younger layer can manufacture an apparent record that the original studies did not establish.

Locality 1's lasting value lies partly in material from multiple phases. Anatomy, tools, and environment can be placed in a sequence and compared. Such a framework explains more than one general age and can accommodate later revisions.

## Several processes brought bones into the cave deposits

Early accounts readily treated the cave as a house occupied over a long period. Bones, tools, and supposed ash found together became the remains of daily life. Subsequent attention to sediments and bone surfaces raised specific problems: how bones arrived, whether tools moved, and whether different objects followed the same processes.

[A 2001 site formation study](https://doi.org/10.1006/jhev.2001.0498) by Goldberg, Weiner, and colleagues combined examination of the surviving section with sediment micromorphology and infrared analysis. It identified water redeposition in some fine sediments. Organic content and dark color did not themselves establish thick accumulations of ash left in place. Cave deposits required an account of their formation before each layer could be given a function in human life.

Carnivores also contributed to bone accumulation. [Spatial and taphonomic analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhevol.2004.01.007) by Boaz, Ciochon, Qinqi Xu, and Jinyi Liu investigated how hyenas and other animals affected damage and distribution. Modifications to human and animal bones needed comparison with gnawing, transport, and digestion. Every fracture could not be attributed to human percussion, and incomplete skulls alone did not establish cannibalism.

[Historical evidence remains disputed] Researchers have differed over the relative contributions of humans, carnivores, and natural transport. Carnivore modification does not remove the manufactured tools from the record. Manufactured tools do not turn every animal bone into human hunting refuse. Different processes need to be tested for coexistence within particular layers and materials.

A cave can be used by different animals at widely separated times. Collapse and water can subsequently rearrange their remains. The concentrated deposit visible today may compress numerous unrelated events. Recognizing that compression is necessary before assessing the duration of human activity and relationships with other animals.

## Tools and animal remains bring daily life closer to investigation

Tools extended Zhoukoudian research beyond anatomical classification. Cores, flakes, and retouched pieces document ways of obtaining sharp edges and working stone. Researchers compare raw materials, flaking traces, dimensions, and positions to investigate manufacture. Some activities also require use wear and experimental comparisons.

Technical interpretation needs to retain the task. An irregular flake may already be suitable for cutting or scraping without being shaped into a form that looks orderly today. A raw material's fracture properties also affect the result. Appearance alone cannot establish a complete absence of planning, and a tool shape need not correspond to only one use.

Animal remains help investigate environments and food, but a species list is not a menu. Finding a large animal initially establishes its presence in the archaeological record. Assessing human processing requires examination of cuts, percussion, body parts, and spatial relationships. Inferring active hunting requires additional consideration of other ways of obtaining a carcass. A processed bone alone does not explain acquisition.

These analyses change the questions asked. Particular resources, depositional processes, and processing traces can be compared without assigning Peking Man an unchanging hunting institution. Behavioral differences become investigable where the record permits. Zhoukoudian's abundance of tools supports such questions without guaranteeing a settled answer to each one.

Regional tool traditions will receive separate treatment in a later installment. The immediate point concerns interpretation of this cave: manufacture establishes human activity, while its form, duration, and environment require evidence beyond the tools themselves.

## Fire research has to distinguish the layers under discussion

The association of Zhoukoudian with fire developed early. Burned bones, heated stones, charcoal, and sediments interpreted as ash connected the cave with human fire use. Repeated access to fire could affect food processing, thermal conditions, and activities, making it central to interpretations of Peking Man. Debate concerned the steps between recognizing heating and establishing human control of fire in place.

In 1998, Weiner, Qinqi Xu, Goldberg, Jinyi Liu, and Bar Yosef investigated evidence associated with Layer 10. They reported burned bones without direct ash or charcoal evidence supporting combustion in place. [Their study](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.281.5374.251) challenged the interpretation of particular material. It did not establish an absence of fire throughout all layers or prove that Peking Man lacked the ability to use it.

New excavation beginning in 2009 recovered material with preserved spatial relationships. [A 2017 study](https://doi.org/10.1086/692501) by Xing Gao, Shuangquan Zhang, Yue Zhang, and Fuyou Chen argued for evidence of fire use in place in Layer 4, while calling for further investigation of lower horizons. Findings in Layer 4 can revise that layer's interpretation without automatically settling every issue concerning Layers 8 to 10.

In 2022, Chao Huang, Jingshu Li, and Xing Gao published X ray diffraction research on heated bone. Combining appearance with mineral structure, they inferred substantial heating in some analyzed material, adding support to fire interpretations at Layer 4. [The original paper](https://doi.org/10.3389/feart.2021.811319) also addressed how burial processes can affect color. Temperature is not a universal boundary separating human fires from natural ones. Fuel conditions, position, and other contextual information still matter.

[Historical evidence remains disputed] Early ash interpretations, the 1998 examination of lower deposits, and new Layer 4 results concern different samples and questions. Judgments must follow the corresponding evidence. Fire use in place, maintenance, repeated use, and the ability to ignite a fire also require separate investigation. Establishing one does not establish all the others or reconstruct a household with fixed daily roles.

## Wartime loss made surviving documentation indispensable

The expansion of Japan's invasion of China in 1937 interrupted sustained excavation at Zhoukoudian. Fieldwork became unsafe, and protecting staff and collections became an immediate concern. In 1941, a major collection of Peking Man fossils held at Peking Union Medical College disappeared amid preparations for protective transfer. The war changed the kinds of material later researchers could examine as well as the pace of work.

Shipwrecks, burial, and seizure have all figured in accounts of the fossils' destination. [Historical evidence remains disputed] These leads have not established a verified chain of evidence locating the missing collection. None should be narrated as a confirmed ending. [The Beijing cultural heritage authority's account](https://wwj.beijing.gov.cn/bjww/wwjzzcslm/1737418/1738090/wwgs/623000/index.html) describes transfer arrangements and the conflicting accounts that followed.

Earlier measurements, photographs, drawings, and casts by Weidenreich and others consequently became indispensable. Casts retain external form; documentation connects specimens with previous investigation. They do not retain every internal structure or molecule in the original bone. Some questions posed by later techniques can no longer be asked of the missing originals. Records reduced the loss without reversing it.

It is also inaccurate to say that every Peking Man fossil disappeared. Early teeth already in Uppsala and material discovered after 1949 still permit work on originals. [The 2018 dental study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-20432-y) reexamined surviving teeth externally and internally, showing how a limited original sample can support new tests.

Excavation resumed after 1949, and important cranial material was found in 1966. Research continued after the loss, with an increased need to specify whether an analysis uses an original, an early cast, or a later digital reconstruction. That collection history affects the scope of measurements made today.

## New methods continue to draw evidence from existing collections

New findings do not always require excavation of another large fossil. Micro CT can reveal internal dental and skeletal structures, while three dimensional comparison makes morphological judgments more explicit. Such methods also require records of scanning, segmentation, and reconstruction. Surfaces in a reconstructed model do not all have equal direct support.

In 2023, researchers identified a hominin parietal fragment among material previously classified as mammalian fossils from Zhoukoudian Locality 15. [The IVPP's research account](https://www.ivpp.cas.cn/xwdt/kyjz/202307/t20230717_6811255.html) explains the role of CT and three dimensional investigation. Locality 15 material cannot simply be added to an individual or chronological sequence from Locality 1. The discovery illustrates how reassessment of collections changes the available sample.

In May 2026, Qiaomei Fu, Zhongyou Wu, E. Andrew Bennett, Song Xing, and colleagues published enamel protein research on six Homo erectus teeth from Zhoukoudian, Hexian, and Sunjiadong. The analyzed material was considered within an approximate 400,000 year timeframe. The six specimens shared identified amino acid variants, one of which had not been observed in the other hominin comparative samples used. [The study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10478-8) added molecular information to anatomical comparisons.

It recovered partial protein sequences without reading six complete ancient human genomes. Such information can test connections among sites without providing a complete pedigree for each individual. The authors also discussed possible ancient genetic exchange involving populations related to Denisovans. This interpretation needs its comparative samples and models; it does not identify a family connecting a particular Peking Man and Denisovan individual.

From prewar casts to surviving teeth and a newly identified bone, excavation, collecting, and analytical conditions have shaped the Zhoukoudian record. Preserving provenance and treatment histories makes new results open to scrutiny. Relating analytical findings to original contexts turns additional information into an account of the past.

## Zhoukoudian provides a reference within China's early human history

Compared with Yuanmou's limited human material and Lantian's key fossils, Zhoukoudian supplies a richer series for examining shared anatomy, individual differences, and activity in different phases. It provides a reference for East Asian Homo erectus and connects anatomical work with tools, animals, and sediments at one locality.

The process has a specific causal structure. Continuing excavation enlarged the sample and enabled comparison. Better field documentation preserved associations that allowed later researchers to test interpretations of life. Sedimentary and experimental work exposed problems in earlier models; new excavation supplied more suitable evidence. Progress depended on additional discoveries and on more precise judgments about them.

That history cannot be extended into a local genealogy in which Peking Man simply became today's residents of Beijing. Ancient classification, modern human origins, and later historical communities are questions at different scales. Finds within present day China belong within the geographical scope of a general history of China. Specific relationships to later populations still require appropriate evidence.

Zhoukoudian entered the World Heritage List in 1987, with protection extending to deposits and their setting. Unexcavated material retains relationships that future work may test. Existing collections can be reassessed while the site itself needs preservation. Both determine the possibilities for further investigation.

Peking Man's life becomes more accessible through these verifiable relationships. Skullcaps clarified anatomy; tools establish manufacture; sediments and bone surfaces constrain reconstructions; fire and protein research supply further questions and evidence. Zhoukoudian contributes a particular record that remains open to reassessment within China's early human history.

## Timeline of discoveries and research

These years date discoveries, research, and protection since the early twentieth century. They are distinct from the ages of the ancient remains.

1. 1921 CE: Zdansky participates in early Zhoukoudian excavation, obtaining dental material important to subsequent study.
2. 1927 CE: Davidson Black proposes the name Sinanthropus pekinensis.
3. 1929 CE: The Cenozoic Research Laboratory is established; Pei Wenzhong discovers a relatively complete Peking Man skullcap on December 2.
4. 1936 CE: Jia Lanpo discovers three Peking Man skullcaps in succession in November.
5. 1937 CE: War interrupts continuing excavation.
6. 1941 CE: A major prewar fossil collection disappears amid preparations for protective transfer.
7. 1949 CE: Excavation resumes and subsequently supplies further material.
8. 1966 CE: Important cranial remains are discovered again at Locality 1.
9. 1987 CE: The Peking Man Site at Zhoukoudian enters the World Heritage List.
10. 1998 CE: Research on Layer 10 challenges evidence for burning in place.
11. 2009 CE: Burial dating of Layers 7 to 10 is published; renewed field excavation begins.
12. 2017 CE: Evidence for fire use in place from new Layer 4 excavation is published.
13. 2022 CE: Analysis of heating in newly excavated bones adds to Layer 4 fire research.
14. 2023 CE: Researchers report a hominin parietal identified in Locality 15 collections.
15. 2026 CE: Enamel protein research on six Homo erectus teeth, including Zhoukoudian specimens, is published.
