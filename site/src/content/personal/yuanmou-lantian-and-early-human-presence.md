Volume I, Early Humans and Prehistoric Societies within China. Chapter 2, Early Humans in China. Section 1.

Yuanmou Man is often introduced with an age of approximately 1.7 million years. The number can suggest a complete scene: people of known appearance and understood habits establishing a camp somewhere in Yunnan. The core human fossil evidence consists of just two upper central incisors. Between those teeth and an account of human life a million years ago lie morphological comparisons, attempts to identify their original context, stratigraphic correlations, and environmental research.

Lantian Man can be equally misleading as a name. The Chenjiawo mandible and Gongwangling cranium came from the same county, but represent different periods. The age of Gongwangling has also undergone substantial revision. The difference between the 1.15 million years in older accounts and the approximately 1.63 million years now commonly cited changes its position in the early Eurasian human record.

These discoveries help establish that early humans reached different parts of what is now China at an early date. Their presence requires evidence from several places. China here defines a modern geographical area; it does not assign a state, ethnicity, or unified culture to its ancient inhabitants. Following [Fossils and Ancient DNA in the Study of Human Origins](/personal/china-history/fossils-ancient-dna-and-human-origins/), this essay turns to particular remains and the history of their interpretation to explain what those sites establish.

## Basins and mountain margins preserved traces of human activity

Yuanmou and Lantian first need to be placed geographically. Yuanmou is a basin in Yunnan near the southeastern margin of the Tibetan Plateau. Lantian lies along the northern foothills of the Qinling Mountains in Shaanxi, at the southern end of the loess region. Far apart, they have different environments and different processes of sediment accumulation. Comparing them helps establish the geographical extent of early human presence without presuming a known route between their inhabitants.

Water mattered practically. Drinking, finding plants, and encountering animal resources all depended partly on its distribution. Rivers, lake margins, and foothills could bring different resources within reach. The archaeological distribution visible today also reflects preservation. Sediments buried teeth, bones, and tools; later erosion exposed layers that geological surveyors could investigate.

A valley rich in finds consequently involves at least two sets of processes. One concerns how people used their surroundings. The other concerns how objects were buried, transported, exposed, and discovered. The first shaped human life; the second shaped the record available for study. Conflating them can turn a place with good preservation into a supposed center of population.

[Environmental research at Yuanmou](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhevol.2008.08.005) used fauna and pollen to describe an alluvial fan near a lake or swamp, with open vegetation interspersed with bushes and woodland. That provides more specific information than a general image of warm forest. It describes available environmental conditions without identifying an individual's meal or sleeping place.

Placing these remains in the Early Pleistocene also requires setting aside the institutions familiar from later history. There are no dynasties, offices, or military organizations to trace here. Relevant questions concern movement, obtaining food, seasonal resources, and persistence in a region. Modern provincial boundaries serve only to locate the evidence.

## Yuanmou was discovered through geological fieldwork

In 1965, geologist Qian Fang discovered two hominin teeth near Shangnabang in Yuanmou, Yunnan. Paleontologist Hu Chengzhi subsequently studied them and named the Yuanmou form of Homo erectus in his 1973 publication. The discovery brought early human material from Yunnan into wider research. [The Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology's account of Hu's career](https://www.ivpp.cas.cn/xwdt/zhxw/201811/t20181103_5157037.html) records his study and publication of the material.

Qian and Hu performed different tasks. Field investigation needed to establish where the finds came from and what sediments and fossils surrounded them. Morphological research needed to determine whether they were human and how they compared with other hominins. Teeth do not arrive with a complete description of their history. A discovery changes understanding when these tasks can be connected, as well as when a rare specimen is recognized.

Yuanmou Man became a familiar name for a region's remote human past. Public memory often retains the locality and its age. Research must still return to the specimens and their setting. How the teeth were assigned to a layer, which tools came from documented excavations, and whether surface finds were distinguished from excavated material all affect the historical account that the name can support.

These distinctions matter to later work. A surface find may have eroded out of its original layer or moved a short distance. Subsequent excavation nearby can test sediments and archaeological occurrences, but cannot automatically restore every detail of an incompletely recorded discovery. Relationships between materials need to be verifiable. Later finds cannot all be assembled into a single reconstruction of the original moment of discovery.

[Historical evidence remains disputed] The association between the Yuanmou incisors and their precise original layer retains limitations. The 2020 dating paper explicitly notes uncertainty about the exact layer in which the teeth were found. This does not invalidate their identification as hominin teeth. It limits an unconditional transfer of one sedimentary age to the specimens. Discovering a human fossil, dating nearby sediments, and establishing that both represent the same event are connected tasks requiring separate evidence. [The original Yuanmou burial dating study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.quageo.2019.101022) preserves that qualification.

## Two incisors allow comparisons without reconstructing a society

Teeth are particularly useful in paleoanthropology. Hard enamel and aspects of dental form can survive after other parts of a jaw have disappeared. At Yuanmou, researchers can examine crown size, structures on the tongue facing surface, and marginal form, comparing them with apes, other members of Homo, and modern humans. Classification should depend on combinations of traits rather than an impression that the teeth are large.

Zhou Guoxing and Hu Chengzhi's [reassessment of the Yuanmou incisors](https://www.ivpp.cas.cn/cbw/gjzdwxb/xbwzxz/200904/t20090428_2386420.html) examined differences from ape teeth and those of later humans. Such comparisons explain why the specimens entered discussions of early humans. Enlarging the comparative sample can subsequently test the precision of an earlier classification. Continued use of a name does not settle every taxonomic detail.

Homo erectus is a classification used to compare fossils with related morphological characteristics. It does not mean that upright walking began with this species, and it cannot replace a description of an individual specimen. With only two incisors, a cautious account can describe affinities with early Homo and broadly defined Homo erectus. A complete skull shape, stature, or brain volume cannot be supplied by the species name.

Nor do the teeth reveal a particular family structure, group size, division of labor, or language. Older general histories sometimes made remote life visible through a complete imagined daily scene. Without distinguishing assumptions, such scenes obscure which details come from objects and which come from analogy. At Yuanmou, the limited human material can establish that early hominins left remains at the locality.

Fire also requires a separate evaluation. Scattered charcoal, discolored bone, or heated stone needs examination alongside combustion features, heating patterns, and sedimentary context before human control can be inferred. Evidence of burning still requires distinctions among natural fires, use of fire, and repeated maintenance of a fire source. Identifying early humans does not establish that Yuanmou's inhabitants regularly made fires for cooking.

## The age of 1.7 million years underwent repeated testing

Yuanmou's age has been disputed. Early paleomagnetic work supported an age of approximately 1.7 million years. In 1983, Liu Dongsheng and Ding Menglin proposed a younger interpretation based on stratigraphic and faunal relationships. Qian Fang responded in 1985, defending an Early Pleistocene placement. Their disagreement concerned disturbance, the use of animal fossils for correlation, and which interval of geomagnetic history the sedimentary record represented. [The 1983 paper](https://www.anthropol.ac.cn/CN/Y1983/V2/I01/40) and [the 1985 response](https://www.anthropol.ac.cn/CN/Y1985/V4/I04/324) present those interpretations.

Stratigraphic correlation was central. Some minerals retain a magnetic direction acquired during sediment formation. Sampling a section produces a sequence of normal and reversed polarity that researchers compare with an established chronology. Matching part of the record to an earlier or later magnetic event can substantially change the inferred age. Sampling density, location, and continuity therefore matter to the correlation.

In 2008, high resolution magnetostratigraphic research by Zhu Rixiang, Richard Potts, and colleagues again supported an age of approximately 1.7 million years. Improved links between the site and its dating sequence allowed a closer test of the Early Pleistocene interpretation. The number should be read as an approximate age, rather than a birth date precise to a particular year.

Another study, published in 2020, used aluminum 26 and beryllium 10 in quartz for burial dating. The measured material was a mineral in the sediments; the instrument did not directly read an age from the teeth. The upper gravel layer yielded an age of approximately 1.54 million years, with an uncertainty of about 0.11 million years including external errors. Combining that result with paleomagnetism, the authors supported a site chronology of approximately 1.7 million years. An upper dated layer and an associated lower horizon can have different ages without contradiction.

Explaining the result requires retaining both the material dated and its relationship to the human remains. Removing those links turns an inference into a supposedly direct measurement. Where the original context is incomplete, other layers and independent methods become particularly important. A related problem appears in a different form at Lantian.

## The name Lantian Man covers two different localities

Lantian provides more material than Yuanmou, but its place name can merge distinct discoveries. A hominin mandible was found at Chenjiawo in 1963, followed by a cranium at Gongwangling in 1964. Accounts of Lantian Man frequently discuss them together. They came from different places, cannot be joined into one individual's head, and need not have belonged to contemporaries simply because they were discovered in successive years.

[A study of the chronology of Chinese hominin sites](https://www.anthropol.ac.cn/EN/article/downloadArticleFile.do?attachType=PDF&id=1022) distinguishes the localities. Chenjiawo is generally placed within a later Middle Pleistocene framework, while Gongwangling's revised chronology places it in the Early Pleistocene. Saying only that Lantian Man lived approximately 1.63 million years ago can incorrectly give the Chenjiawo mandible the same age. The specific locality matters.

The Gongwangling cranium provides more cranial structures for comparison, although preservation is imperfect. Deformation and erosion after burial altered some measurable features. Treating a reconstructed outline as an unmodified original can overstate measurement certainty. Classification requires distinguishing directly preserved structures from those compared after physical assembly or digital processing.

In 2022, Lei Pan, Song Xing, Wu Liu, and collaborators used micro CT and three dimensional methods to investigate Gongwangling teeth. Internal structures, including the junction between enamel and dentine, contributed further evidence for comparison within broadly defined Homo erectus. [The dental study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35688108/) shows how an existing specimen can yield new information without another skull being discovered.

A discovery therefore remains open to investigation after a specimen enters a collection. Better imaging, larger comparative samples, and renewed fieldwork can revise earlier interpretations. Lantian Man may be one familiar name, but research addresses particular specimens, multiple localities, and records that do not occupy the same point in time.

## Missing sediment changed Gongwangling's position in time

Gongwangling was long assigned an age of approximately 1.15 million years. Reinvestigation by Zhu Zhaoyu, Robin Dennell, Huang Weiwen, and colleagues identified a stratigraphic hiatus. Sediments previously treated as a continuous sequence did not preserve all the intervening time. The fossil horizon needed correlation with older paleosol units. The research appeared online in 2014 and in a 2015 journal issue. [The revised dating paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25456822/) proposed approximately 1.63 million years.

A hiatus resembles missing pages in a book. Adjacent surviving pages need not describe adjacent days. Sediment may not have accumulated during an interval, or erosion may later have removed it. Treating that gap as a complete sequence can place older material in a younger chronological framework. Correcting the correlation changes the fossil's inferred age.

The revision narrowed the temporal separation between early human fossils and some northern stone tool records. A relatively late age for Gongwangling could no longer be used to place all earlier activity beyond the local skeletal record. Narrowing a gap does not remove every gap, however. Some archaeological sites lack human remains, and their toolmakers still require separate identification.

Burial dating provided an additional test in 2017. The study obtained approximately 1.82 million years, with an uncertainty of about 0.12 million years, from the top of the gravels beneath Gongwangling's loess and paleosols. [The original paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.quageo.2017.04.004) used that age as a stratigraphic control. It supports an older basal sequence without directly dating the cranium to 1.82 million years. This result and the approximately 1.63 million year interpretation of the fossil horizon cannot be substituted for one another.

The revision changes the fossil's place in comparisons without establishing it as the ancestor of every subsequent East Asian population. Earlier occurrence alone does not demonstrate parentage. Relationships between Gongwangling and later Zhoukoudian specimens need morphological, chronological, and geographical evidence, which the following installment will examine more closely.

## Shangchen added earlier traces of activity near Lantian

Research in the Lantian area changed again in 2018. Zhu Zhaoyu and colleagues published the Shangchen stone tool sequence, extending evidence of local hominin activity to approximately 2.12 million years ago. Artifact horizons occurred across a long interval within loess and paleosols. [The Shangchen study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0299-4) expanded the area's significance beyond the age of one cranium.

Shangchen's evidence consists of stone artifacts. Researchers evaluate striking platforms, flake scars, and context when assessing manufacture, then place artifact horizons within a chronology. Such evidence supports human activity without providing a skull or teeth of the corresponding age. Gongwangling's anatomical form cannot simply be projected hundreds of thousands of years earlier onto the Shangchen toolmakers.

A long interval between the earliest and latest tools also does not establish uninterrupted residence by one population. Different layers may record activity separated by many generations. Intervals without artifacts may reflect absence, failed preservation, or incomplete discovery. A long sequence widens the window of observation without filling all the missing details of life within it.

Shangchen requires models of Eurasian dispersal to accommodate an earlier East Asian record. If a model permits arrival only after a particular date, reliable older activity requires revision. The species involved, intervening regions, and number of dispersal episodes remain separate questions. An earlier chronology does not by itself establish a route.

Shangchen and Gongwangling are consequently useful together. One supplies an older sequence of technological activity; the other supplies human anatomy for comparison. They contribute different parts of a regional record. Geographical proximity does not make them one living site.

## Nihewan brought northern seasonality into the discussion

Yunnan and the northern Qinling foothills alone might suggest a story confined to relatively mild environments. Sites in the Nihewan Basin in Hebei require consideration of more northerly conditions. In 2004, Zhu Rixiang, Richard Potts, Xie Fei, and colleagues placed Majuangou's lowest artifact horizon at approximately 1.66 million years. Near 40 degrees north, the site documents early activity at higher northeastern Asian latitudes. [The Majuangou paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature02829) connected tools, animal remains, and magnetostratigraphy.

Higher latitude raises a practical issue: resources change through the year. Available plant parts, animal movements, and drinking water can vary by season. Entering during a warm interval and sustaining residence throughout a year demand different capacities. Evidence of activity does not alone establish every adaptation needed for severe winters.

Published in 2013, research at Shangshazui also placed early Nihewan activity within a discussion of approximately 1.6 to 1.7 million years. [The Shangshazui study](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep02403), alongside other basin sites, provides a record assembled from multiple localities. Questions can move beyond whether humans reached the north to the environmental stages in which they appeared and their use of different places.

Early tools record manufacture and activity without supplying a human fossil for every assemblage. Similarity in age to Yuanmou does not assign the makers the same dental anatomy. Relatively simple tool forms do not establish identical behavior either. Raw materials, tasks, and preservation can all affect the surviving assemblage.

From Yuanmou to Nihewan, the evidence expands the geographical and environmental range under consideration. People sought resources in different settings, leaving traces in different depositional systems. An explanation for East Asia needs to account for those differences rather than extending one imagined local way of life across the entire region.

## New results require qualifications when describing the earliest evidence

By 2026, describing Yuanmou without qualification as China's earliest humans no longer adequately reflects research. Earlier stone tools and earlier human fossils need separate comparisons. Within each category, accounts should identify whether material was found in place, what was dated, and how the chronology was tested. A ranking condensed into one sentence conceals those differences.

Xihoudu in Shanxi illustrates the issue. In 2020, Shen Guanjun, Wang Yiren, and colleagues reported a burial age of approximately 2.43 million years, with an uncertainty of about 0.06 million years. They also addressed whether stones affected by river abrasion could be recognized as artifacts. [The Xihoudu study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anthro.2020.102790) combines claims about manufacture and chronological association. [Historical evidence remains disputed] Discussion of manufacture and transport belongs alongside the dating result. Selecting only the oldest number cannot establish the toolmakers' species.

In February 2026, Hua Tu, Zhongping Lai, Darryl Granger, Guanjun Shen, and collaborators proposed approximately 1.77 million years for the hominin cranium bearing horizon at Yunxian. The reported uncertainty of 0.08 million years is one standard deviation of internal error. [The original study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13267281/) measured quartz samples associated with the fossil horizon, proposing a new chronology for Xuetangliangzi. It changes comparisons with Gongwangling and other early human fossils.

[Historical evidence remains disputed] A 2025 morphological and phylogenetic study connected the reconstructed Yunxian 2 cranium with a lineage associated with Homo longi and Denisovans. This did not directly recover an ancient genome from that cranium. [The morphological study](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.ado9202) and the 2026 dating paper require consideration of their respective data and assumptions. The new age must be read alongside earlier chronologies and relationship interpretations. A proposed older age does not erase previous research or settle every classification and affinity question. Here Yunxian enters the comparison of early records; its detailed anatomy and debates concerning Middle Pleistocene populations belong in later installments.

The earliest evidence therefore requires a complete description: which material, within which area, according to which study. New material and revised dates can change the answer. Each locality retains its value without needing to hold an age record.

## Separate discoveries begin to form a regional history

The discoveries changed understanding through a traceable sequence. Field surveys supplied human remains beyond a few familiar localities. Renewed stratigraphic work changed their chronological placement. Older tools and northern sites then required explanation across a longer interval and more environments. The larger account emerged through constraints between records, rather than a ladder of place names representing successive evolutionary steps.

Points on a map do not establish migration routes. Yunnan, Shaanxi, Hebei, and Hubei preserve remains from potentially different periods, activities, and populations. Connecting them requires material from intervening areas, comparable dates, and sufficient anatomical or technological evidence. Tools at two sites bring both into the human activity record without deciding who moved from one place to the other.

Other Eurasian sites, including Dmanisi, provide a larger scale for comparison. Dmanisi in Georgia shows how different layers and human fossils can document repeated use of a locality. [Ferring and colleagues' study](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1106638108) supplies a reference for early Eurasian dispersal. Comparing East Asian evidence with it tests timing and conditions without placing distant individuals in a known family.

Early human presence cannot automatically be extended into a continuous history of agriculture, civilization, or states. The interval between Yuanmou and Yangshao includes numerous population and social changes that require their own evidence. Early discoveries give the geographical setting of Chinese history greater temporal depth. Later political communities still have particular histories of formation. Keeping evidence within its appropriate period makes long term change explicable.

Two Yuanmou incisors bring one locality into human origins research. The Gongwangling cranium adds anatomical comparisons. Shangchen and Nihewan expand the range of activity that can be observed. Together they make visible a variety of changing environments that early humans encountered in East Asia. The surviving objects are limited, but their significance becomes clearer as their positions in place and time are tested.

## Timeline of discoveries and research

The following years date discoveries or publications. Ages expressed in millions of years in the main text refer to the ancient remains.

1. 1963 CE: A hominin mandible is discovered at Chenjiawo in Lantian.
2. 1964 CE: A hominin cranium is discovered at Gongwangling in Lantian.
3. 1965 CE: Qian Fang discovers two hominin incisors at Yuanmou in Yunnan.
4. 1973 CE: Hu Chengzhi publishes the dental study naming the Yuanmou form of Homo erectus.
5. 1983 CE: Liu Dongsheng and Ding Menglin publish a different age interpretation of the Yuanmou locality.
6. 1985 CE: Qian Fang responds, defending a chronology of approximately 1.7 million years.
7. 2004 CE: The Majuangou study places the lowest tool horizon at approximately 1.66 million years.
8. 2008 CE: High resolution Yuanmou magnetostratigraphy supports approximately 1.7 million years.
9. 2015 CE: The Gongwangling revision proposing approximately 1.63 million years appears in its journal issue, following online publication in 2014.
10. 2017 CE: Burial dating of underlying Gongwangling gravels tests the older stratigraphic framework.
11. 2018 CE: The Shangchen study places its earliest artifact horizon at approximately 2.12 million years.
12. 2020 CE: The Yuanmou burial dating paper appears in its journal issue; the Xihoudu paper proposes approximately 2.43 million years in the same year.
13. 2022 CE: Micro CT research on Gongwangling's internal dental anatomy is published.
14. 2026 CE: The Yunxian burial dating study proposes approximately 1.77 million years for the fossil horizon.

## Researchers and their connections to the evidence

<p>Qian Fang worked on the Yuanmou discovery and field stratigraphy. Hu Chengzhi studied and named the incisors. Their work connected field records with identification of human remains. Zhou Guoxing and Hu subsequently compared the dental morphology further.</p>

<p>Liu Dongsheng, Ding Menglin, and Qian Fang offered different interpretations of Yuanmou stratigraphy and age. Their connection is a research debate, rather than an ancestral relationship between ancient people.</p>

<p>Zhu Rixiang, Richard Potts, and collaborators investigated magnetostratigraphy and early activity at Yuanmou and Nihewan. Zhu Zhaoyu, Robin Dennell, Huang Weiwen, and collaborators worked on the Gongwangling revision and Shangchen sequence. Zhu Rixiang and Zhu Zhaoyu are different researchers.</p>

<p>Lei Pan, Song Xing, Wu Liu, and collaborators studied Gongwangling dental morphology with micro CT. The author groups differ between publications.</p>
